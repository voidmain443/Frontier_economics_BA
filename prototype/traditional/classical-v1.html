How economics is taught

Almost every department teaches the same sequence: principles, then mathematics, then micro, then macro, then econometrics, then the field courses. Most graduates leave remembering the diagrams and not why they were stacked in that order. This page rebuilds the reasons, one sheet at a time.

Read it as a textbook. Every derivation has an Anatomy block: the reasoning in steps, and the graphs and equations it is built from, each marked with the course that made it. Select Assemble in 3D on any block to watch that derivation put together in space, step by step; drag to turn it. A figure can also be opened on its own with Look inside.

1Principles

The whole map, drawn once at low resolution.

Principles is a survey. In one or two semesters it touches nearly every idea the rest of the degree returns to: scarcity, trade, markets, prices, government, inflation, unemployment. Little is derived carefully. The goal is a map with every region labeled, so that later courses have somewhere to put what they prove.

Mankiw’s Principles of Economics, the most widely assigned introductory text, opens with ten principles. The first is “people face trade-offs.”
At Seoul National University the required core is micro theory, macro theory, economic history, economic statistics and mathematics for economists, all in the second year after two semesters of principles. The field courses open in the third year.

The order is the logic of the whole degree in miniature. It starts with a single trade-off, scales it to a whole economy, explains why people trade, shows how a market sets a price, asks when the market gets it wrong, and only then steps back to count the economy as a whole. The first half becomes microeconomics; the second half becomes macroeconomics.

The circular flow introduces the cast. Households own labor and capital and buy goods; firms hire labor and capital and sell goods. Every later model is this picture with more detail: microeconomics zooms into the boxes, macroeconomics measures the arrows.

Qd(P) = Qs(P)

Supply and demand are introduced as facts about markets, not derived. Shifts are taught as rules. Where the curves come from is postponed to microeconomics, and how the price travels to the new equilibrium is usually never asked.

The macro half

The second half changes scale. Instead of one market it counts the whole economy, then asks why some economies grow, what money is, and why output and employment fluctuate. The vocabulary arrives now; the models that connect it arrive two years later.

Uses algebra and graphs. Feeds every later course: it supplies the vocabulary of the whole degree.

What it leaves out. Where the curves come from, every variable held fixed behind them, and time.

2Mathematics for economists

The language the rest of the degree is written in.

Between principles and the intermediate courses sits a mathematics course, sometimes a separate class and sometimes the first weeks of intermediate micro. Its content is narrow and chosen for what comes next, and its order follows the questions economics asks: what is marginal, what is best, what is best among the allowed, how the best moves, and how a system evolves.

ℒ(x, y, λ) = f(x, y) + λ (c − g(x, y))   ⟹   ∇f = λ ∇g
The multiplier λ has its own economic meaning: how much the objective would rise if the constraint were relaxed by one unit. Economists call it a shadow price.

Every intermediate model has this shape: an agent chooses something to make an objective as large as possible, subject to a constraint. Students who learn it as a procedure find the next two years a list of unrelated tricks. Students who learn it as a picture find that consumer choice, cost minimization and general equilibrium are the same picture three times.

Uses single-variable calculus. Feeds microeconomics (optimization), macroeconomics (dynamics), econometrics (linear algebra and probability).

What it leaves out. Usually, the economics. The tools arrive before the questions that need them.

3Microeconomics

Individual choices, and how they meet in markets.

Hal Varian’s Intermediate Microeconomics and Nicholson and Snyder’s Intermediate Microeconomics and Its Application follow nearly this order.

Intermediate microeconomics is built in a fixed order, and each step keeps the previous model and adds one thing. One consumer: preferences, then a budget, then demand. The same consumer across time and under risk. One firm: technology, then cost, then supply. Markets: competition as the benchmark, then market power, then strategy. All markets at once. Then the list of ways the result can fail.

Game theory, a course of its own

Oligopoly is where price taking breaks down: each firm’s best choice depends on what the others do. Most departments then teach game theory as a separate course, because the same tool organizes far more than oligopoly. Its order runs from static games to dynamic ones, and from complete to incomplete information.

All markets at once

Uses constrained optimization and comparative statics from chapter 2. Feeds industrial organization, public economics, labor, international trade, finance.

What it leaves out. Most of the agents. One consumer and one firm stand in for millions, and the diagrams hold every other market still.

4Macroeconomics

Aggregates, and what happens to them over time.

Macroeconomics changes the object of study. Instead of a consumer or a market it studies the whole economy through a handful of totals, output, the price level, unemployment and the interest rate, and asks how they move. Its order is: measure, then the long run with flexible prices, then money, then the short run with sticky prices, then expectations, then policy, then the open economy.

k̇ = s f(k) − (n + δ) k
The expectations-augmented Phillips curve is usually credited to Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps in the late 1960s, before the inflation of the 1970s made it hard to ignore.
Carlin and Soskice, Macroeconomics: Institutions, Instability, and the Financial System, builds the undergraduate course around the three-equation model.

Uses dynamics from chapter 2 and optimization from chapter 3. Feeds money and banking, international finance, public economics, finance.

What it leaves out. Differences between people. A single representative household stands in for an economy of rich and poor, borrowers and lenders.

5Econometrics

Measuring what the models claim.

Econometrics turns the models’ relationships into numbers. Its order follows one technique and one worry: first least squares and what makes it trustworthy, then everything that can break the link between a correlation and a cause, then the designs that repair it.

Joshua Angrist’s 14.32 at MIT reverses the order: randomized trials and potential outcomes come first, so every later method is read as an attempt to recover an experiment.
β̂ = (XᵀX)−1Xᵀy
Joshua Angrist and Jörn-Steffen Pischke called the shift toward research designs “the credibility revolution.” Angrist and Guido Imbens shared the 2021 Nobel memorial prize for it with David Card.

Uses linear algebra and probability from chapter 2. Feeds every empirical field course and the senior thesis.

What it leaves out. Most of the variables. Every regression is a slice through a higher-dimensional cloud.

6Field courses

The same tools, pointed at particular objects.

In the final years the sequence branches. Each field course takes the tools of the core and applies them to one part of the economy, usually by adding one agent or one friction to a model the student already knows. Read down the last column and the pattern is plain. Trade adds a second country, money and banking adds an instrument, public economics turns the state into a decision maker, labor adds the time it takes to find a match, finance prices risk, behavioral economics rewrites the agent, and development and history add the institutions and the centuries that the core holds fixed. Each field relaxes one of the core’s simplifications, and each is taught as if the others did not exist.

Uses the whole core. Feeds research, policy work, and the thesis.

What it leaves out. The connections between fields. Each is taught as its own branch, and the student assembles the tree alone.

7The record

Five episodes in which the core models met real data.

Each tank, valve and dial of the machine beside the unfolded page is an equation from the core courses. The capital tank is the Solow model, the income tank and its valves are IS–LM, the inflation column is the expectations-augmented Phillips curve, the exchange valve and the reserve tank are the open economy, and the double well is a bank run. This chapter runs that machine on the historical record. As each episode crosses the reading line, its chart advances by year or month, and when the page is unfolded the same date drives the machine.

The series come from the Bank of Korea (ECOS), Statistics Korea (KOSIS), the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED) and the US Energy Information Administration, retrieved on 29 October 2026. Codes and transformations are listed in data/SOURCES.md.

Korea, 1960–2025: filling the capital tank

In 1960 gross fixed capital formation was 11 percent of Korea’s GDP. It reached 26 percent by 1970, 33 percent by 1980 and a peak of 38 percent in 1991, and it has stayed near 30 percent since the 1997 crisis. Over the same 65 years real GDP per person rose about 36-fold.

In the Solow model a higher saving rate raises the steady state k* and produces a long period of fast growth while capital accumulates toward it. Growth then slows as diminishing returns set in. Korea’s output per worker grew 6.6 percent a year from 1963 to 1973 and 6.4 percent from 1983 to 1993, then 4.7, 2.7 and 1.5 percent in the three decades after. The machine’s faucet follows each year’s investment share and its drain follows population growth plus depreciation, so the tank fills quickly and then levels off.

The model leaves an open question, and economists argued over it. Alwyn Young (1995) measured how much of East Asia’s growth came from adding capital and workers rather than from rising productivity, and Paul Krugman drew the conclusion that such growth would slow. Growth accounting is the tool for that argument: whatever the tank cannot explain is the residual, technology.

United States, 1960–1986: the Phillips curve moves

In the 1960s American unemployment fell from 6.7 percent in 1961 to 3.5 percent in 1969 while inflation rose from about 1 percent to 5.4 percent. The points lie along a downward-sloping curve, the trade-off that textbooks of the time described.

The 1970s broke it. Inflation reached 11.0 percent in 1974 with unemployment at 5.6 percent, and 13.5 percent in 1980 with unemployment at 7.2 percent. Then the Volcker disinflation: unemployment rose to 9.7 percent in 1982 and inflation fell to 3.2 percent by 1983. The dashed line is expected inflation. From 1978 it comes from the Michigan survey, which rose to 9.5 percent in 1979 and fell to 3.2 percent in 1983; before that it is the average of the two previous years. The short-run curve sits at the level of expected inflation, and it moved with expectations.

In the machine, the inflation column follows the data and the ring around it is expected inflation. When the column rises above the ring, the ring follows it upward, and the trade-off of the 1960s has gone.

Korea, 1997: the trilemma

In June 1996 the won averaged 798 to the dollar and gross foreign reserves were $36.6 billion. Through 1997 the central bank sold dollars to hold the won steady while foreign banks stopped rolling over short-term loans to Korean banks. By October the won had slipped to 922. In November the government requested support from the IMF, and the won averaged 1,026. It averaged 1,484 in December and 1,707 in January 1998. Gross reserves fell to $20.4 billion in December, and the reserves that could actually be used were far lower.

With capital free to leave, the central bank could not hold the exchange rate and also set its own interest rate. Once the won floated, the call rate was raised from about 13 percent to a monthly average of 21 percent in December and 25 percent in January. The current account, which had been in deficit, went into surplus as imports collapsed. Reserves were rebuilt to $74 billion by the end of 1999, and the call rate came back below 5 percent.

2008: a run without depositors

The TED spread is the gap between the rate at which banks lend to each other for three months and the rate on Treasury bills. It measures how much banks fear lending to one another. It averaged 0.4 percentage points in January 2007, rose to 1.3 in August 2007 when the first funds froze, and reached 4.1 in the week of 17 October 2008, a month after Lehman Brothers failed.

No depositors queued outside banks. The run came from wholesale lenders: banks that had financed long-term assets with short-term borrowing from other institutions lost that funding at once. The logic is that of Diamond and Dybvig, a second equilibrium that exists whenever illiquid assets are funded with money that can leave. The Federal Reserve cut its rate from 5.25 percent to almost zero and lent against collateral as lender of last resort. The Bank of Korea cut from 5.25 to 2 percent as the won fell from about 930 to more than 1,400 per dollar.

2021–2026: the valve turned

The Bank of Korea held its base rate at 0.5 percent from May 2020 and raised it for the first time in August 2021, to 0.75 percent. Inflation peaked at 6.3 percent in July 2022 in Korea and at 9.0 percent in June 2022 in the United States. By January 2023 the base rate was 3.5 percent, and it stayed there until October 2024.

The transmission chapter of the macro course can be read off the chart. The rate on new household loans rose from 2.8 percent in May 2020 to 5.6 percent in December 2022. Seoul apartment prices, which had risen through 2021, fell about 10 percent from mid-2022 to a low in May 2024. Inflation was back near 2 percent in 2024. In the latest data, for September 2026, the base rate is 3.0 percent and Seoul apartment prices stand above their 2022 level.

What the machine leaves out. Almost everything that made these episodes hard: who held the debt, which banks were connected, what people believed and why. The machine shows the mechanism each course teaches. The record shows how much happens outside it.

What the sequence leaves out

The staircase works. Each course keeps the last one and adds one thing: choice, then markets, then aggregates, then time, then measurement. But each course is taught through cross-sections: a curve with income held fixed, a market with time removed, an economy with one representative household. The whole is rarely shown. Students learn to move the curves without learning what the curves are slices of.

Unfold the page, and the slices are put back: every curve becomes the shadow of an instrument that moves by its own dynamics, the instruments assemble into one machine, and the machine runs on the historical record. The next part of this project rebuilds the sequence around that idea.
