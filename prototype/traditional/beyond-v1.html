Beyond the flat page

The classical sequence explains a world of representative agents meeting in markets. Many questions economists now ask are about structure instead: who is connected to whom, how unequal sizes are, and how real people play. These need tools that a two-dimensional graph does not have.

1Six degrees

A few shortcuts make a large world small.

In 1967 Stanley Milgram asked people in Nebraska and Kansas to pass a letter, through acquaintances, to a stranger in Massachusetts. The letters that arrived took about six steps on average. In 1998 Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz showed why this is unsurprising. Start with a ring in which each person knows only the nearest neighbours, so distances are long and friends of friends know each other. Rewire a small fraction of ties at random. Average distance collapses long before local clustering does.

For economics this structure matters more than any average. Mark Granovetter (1973) found that people often hear about jobs through acquaintances rather than close friends: the weak ties are the shortcuts. Technologies, prices and panics travel the same way. A representative household has no neighbours, so it cannot show any of this.

2Chains, not cores

The shape of a network decides what can travel through it.

Peter Bearman, James Moody and Katherine Stovel (2004) mapped the romantic relationships among students at one American high school over eighteen months. Instead of a dense core of popular students, most of those who were connected were linked in one long, sparse, chain-like structure with short branches, close to a spanning tree. In such a structure there are few redundant paths: breaking a single tie can split it, and a single new tie can join two distant parts. An average count of partners per student says nothing about this.

3Power laws

When there is no typical size, an average misleads.

Vilfredo Pareto noticed in the 1890s that the number of people with income above x falls roughly like a power of x. The same shape describes city sizes (Zipf’s law; Gabaix 1999 explains why) and the sizes of American firms (Axtell 2001). In a network where newcomers prefer to link to people who are already well connected (Barabási and Albert 1999), the number of people with k ties falls like k−3: a few hubs, and a long tail of everyone else.

Xavier Gabaix (2011) argued that this matters for macroeconomics. If firm sizes follow a power law, shocks to the largest firms do not average out, and they move total output. The representative firm of chapter 3 cannot carry that idea. A distribution can.

4Games real people play

Nash equilibrium predicts what perfectly rational players do. People reason a few steps, and care about fairness.

Keynes (1936) compared investing to a newspaper beauty contest in which you win by picking the face others will pick. In the experimental version everyone chooses a number from 0 to 100, and the winner is closest to two-thirds of the average. The only Nash equilibrium is 0. In Rosemarie Nagel’s (1995) experiments, first-round guesses averaged in the mid-thirties: most players reason one or two steps and stop. When others reason only a little, guessing 0 loses.

In the ultimatum game one player proposes how to split a sum and the other accepts or rejects; a rejection leaves both with nothing. Equilibrium with selfish players predicts the smallest possible offer, always accepted. Across many studies, summarized by Colin Camerer (2003), offers of 40 to 50 percent are the most common, and offers below about 20 percent are rejected about half the time. Behavioral economics (Part 1, chapter 6) begins from results like these.

5Contagion between banks

The same network can be robust and fragile.

In 2008 a loss at a few institutions spread through the claims banks held on one another. Daron Acemoglu, Asuman Ozdaglar and Alireza Tahbaz-Salehi (2015) showed that a densely connected system spreads a small loss thin and absorbs it. A loss larger than the system’s combined buffers is instead carried to every bank. A sparse ring absorbs a large shock somewhere along the line and loses a few banks to a small one. Which network is safer depends on the size of the shock.

6Projects rebuilding the textbook

Teaching economics off the flat page, in several directions at once.

  • CORE EconThe Economy 2.0, Economy, Society, and Public Policy, Doing EconomicsStarts each unit from a real problem (inequality, climate, innovation) and teaches models as tools for it. It brings in power, institutions and empirical work from the first week. Free online.
  • QuantEconLectures in Python and Julia, founded by Thomas Sargent and John StachurskiEvery model is code the learner runs: dynamic programming, Markov chains, heterogeneous agents, networks. Computation replaces the closed-form special case.
  • EconGraphsInteractive graphs for intermediate microeconomicsEvery textbook diagram becomes something the reader can move, so a shift is seen as a cause rather than memorized as a rule.
  • The Evolution of TrustNicky Case’s explorable explanation of the repeated prisoner’s dilemmaGame theory taught by playing it, with populations of strategies that evolve. The same author’s Parable of the Polygons does Schelling’s segregation model.
  • Our World in DataLong-run data on living conditions, with the research behind itThe record behind growth, health, poverty and climate, drawn so that it can be read without a course in statistics.
  • Social and Economic NetworksMatthew O. Jackson’s book and courseNetworks as an economic object: how ties form, how information and behavior spread, why position in a network matters for outcomes.
  • Complexity ExplorerThe Santa Fe Institute’s courses on complex systemsAgent-based models, scaling and power laws, nonequilibrium dynamics: the tools of complexity economics (W. Brian Arthur and others).
  • Causal Inference: The MixtapeScott Cunningham; read with The Effect by Nick Huntington-Klein (theeffectbook.net)The credibility revolution as a textbook: research designs first, with code.
  • Exploring EconomicsA pluralist platform from students and researchersPlaces the neoclassical sequence beside other schools (post-Keynesian, institutional, ecological, feminist and others) so that a reader can compare how each frames a question.
  • Marginal Revolution UniversityShort videos by Tyler Cowen and Alex TabarrokPrinciples in pieces small enough to reorder, with applications first.
  • MIT OpenCourseWareThe full sequence studied in Part 1, openThe classical staircase itself, lecture by lecture: the baseline that the projects above reorganize.

None of these projects throws the classical sequence away. Each takes it off the page in its own way: CORE starts from problems, QuantEcon makes the models runnable, the explorables make them moveable, the network and complexity courses add structure, and the causal-inference books add evidence. Part 3 proposes how to put these together.
