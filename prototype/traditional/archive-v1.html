A new archive

How I would teach classical and modern economics together, based on the curriculum and the ontology built in Parts 1 and 2. The archive is arranged by what economics studies, so that a learner can enter from an agent, a market or a question, and every course is one door among many.

1Explain the ontology first

Every model is a choice of which agents and which markets to hold still.

Economics studies a small set of things. Agents decide: households, firms, workers, banks, governments, central banks, other countries. Markets are where agents meet: goods, labor, money, capital, foreign exchange. Institutions set the rules they meet under: property, contracts, money, regulation. Mechanisms say how outcomes follow: optimization, equilibrium, strategic interaction, diffusion through networks, learning.

A textbook graph is a cross-section of this space. The supply and demand diagram keeps one market and two kinds of agent and holds everything else fixed. IS–LM keeps two markets and one of each agent. Telling the learner which slice is being cut, and from what, is the first thing an archive should do. Part 1 shows each cut being assembled from its parts.

Taught this way, the modern fields are not a separate economics. Each removes one simplification the core made so that the core could be drawn on a page. The classical sequence stays as base camp: optimization and equilibrium, done carefully, are what every relaxation is measured against.

3How to study: three ways through

Paths, not only courses.

By a question: why are some countries rich?

  1. the frontier: what an economy can produce
  2. the classical economy: firms and households, added up
  3. Solow: capital accumulates, then levels off
  4. Korea, 1960–2025: the tank on real data
  5. development and economic history: institutions and centuries

By an agent: the household

  1. choice and demand
  2. choice over time: the Euler equation
  3. consumption in macro and the IS equation
  4. finance: the same equation prices assets
  5. behavioral: when the household is not that consumer

By a market: labor

  1. the firm’s costs and its demand for labor
  2. the labor market in the classical economy
  3. aggregate supply with sticky wages
  4. labor economics: search, human capital, discrimination
  5. instruments: measuring the return to schooling
