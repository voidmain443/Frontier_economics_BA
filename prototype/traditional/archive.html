A new archive

This is how I would teach classical and modern economics together. Part 1 drew the economy as a staircase of slices, and Part 2 drew the structure those slices leave out. An archive can hold both if it is arranged by what economics studies rather than by course titles. A learner can then enter from an agent, a market or a question, and every course becomes one door among many.

Fold the page with the control in the corner when you reach the plan of the archive: it rises into the building it describes, and every name in it is a door.

1What economics studies

1Every model is a choice of which agents and which markets to hold still.

Economics studies a small set of things.

  • Agents decide: households, firms, banks, governments, central banks, other countries.
  • Markets are where agents meet: goods, labor, money, capital, foreign exchange, and the land and air that have no market until one is made.
  • Institutions set the rules they meet under: property, contracts, regulation, taxes, the rules of policy, and the networks that decide who can reach whom.
  • Methods say how outcomes follow: optimization, equilibrium, strategic interaction, measurement, computation, and history.

A textbook figure is a cross-section of this space. The supply-and-demand diagram of Figure 1.1 keeps one market and two kinds of agent, and holds everything else still. IS–LM in Figure 6.2 keeps two markets and one of each agent. Telling the learner which slice is being cut, and from what, is the first thing an archive should do. Part 1 did it figure by figure, under the words held fixed.

2The archive, as a plan

2The courses become routes through one building.

Arrange the four layers as the floors of a building. Methods are the foundation, because every other room is built with them. Institutions stand on the methods, markets on the institutions, and the agents on top, where they decide. Each room holds what one subject knows about one thing, and each is taught somewhere: households in microeconomics, the rules of policy in macroeconomics, networks in Part 2.

Folded, the plan rises into the building it describes: each layer to its own floor, the agents standing on the top floor. Every name in the building is a door, and following one opens the course behind it. A course is one route through this building. The classical sequence of Part 1 is one of them: it enters at one market, climbs to the household and the firm, goes down to strategy and equilibrium in the foundation, comes back up through the markets of macroeconomics, returns to the foundation to measure, and visits every floor in the field courses. A problem-first course such as CORE’s enters at a question, wherever it lands in the building, and goes down only as far as it needs.

An archive arranged by what economics studies lets the classical sequence be one route among several, without throwing it away.

Taught this way, the modern fields are not a separate economics. Each removes one simplification that the core made so that it could be drawn on a page. The classical sequence stays as the foundation: optimization and equilibrium, done carefully, are what every relaxation is measured against.

4Three ways through

4Paths, not only courses.

By a question: why are some countries rich?

  1. all markets at once: what an economy can produce, and when that is efficient
  2. Solow: capital accumulates, then levels off, with Korea’s record beside it
  3. development: institutions, and capital that goes to those who have it
  4. economic history: before 1800, progress made more people
  5. no representative firm: a few giants and a long tail

By an agent: the household

  1. choice and demand
  2. choice over time and across states: the Euler equation
  3. consumption in macro, simplified into the Keynesian cross
  4. finance: the same tangency prices assets
  5. behavioral economics and games real people play: when the household is not that consumer

By a market: labor

  1. the firm’s costs, and its demand for labor
  2. unemployment and expectations in macro
  3. labor economics: who sets the wage, and what a minimum wage does
  4. instruments: measuring a curve that never stands still
  5. who trades with whom: jobs found through weak ties

5The textbooks

5Every door, and what to read next.

The archive is a proposal, not a finished building. Its rooms are the courses that already exist, read through the slices they cut; what it adds is the plan that shows how they stand on one another.
