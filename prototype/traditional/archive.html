A new archive

This is how I would teach classical and modern economics together. Part 1 drew the economy as a staircase of figures, and Part 2 showed why those figures are no longer enough. An archive can hold both if it is arranged by what a textbook actually teaches, which is models. Each model can be placed on one wheel of the whole economy, by what it moves, what it holds still and what it leaves out. A learner can then enter from a question, an agent, a market, an assumption or a method, and every course becomes one way through.

Fold the page with the control in the corner. The wheel this part describes is built beside the page, ring by ring. Each figure then puts it in a state: the parts a model moves keep moving, the parts it holds stop where they are, and the parts it leaves out empty to their outline. The name of every cell is a door.

1What economics studies

1The economy as one wheel, and at its hub the methods that read it.

Economics studies a small set of things in the economy.

  • Agents decide: households, firms, banks, governments, central banks, other countries.
  • Markets are where agents meet and something is exchanged: goods, labor, money, capital, foreign exchange, and the land and air that have no market until one is made.
  • Institutions set the rules they meet under: property, contracts, regulation, taxes, the rules of policy, and the networks that decide who can reach whom.
  • Flows run between them: spending and goods, work and wages, saving and lending, taxes and government purchases, exports and their payments, emissions. Totals such as output and employment are sums of these flows.

It reads them with a small set of methods: optimization, equilibrium, strategic interaction, measurement, computation and history. Methods are of another kind. They are not in the economy. They are the tools a model uses to say how the rest moves.

Arrange the four layers as the rings of one wheel, the circular flow that Frank Knight drew in 1933 as a “wheel of wealth”. The agents stand on the rim, where everything that circulates starts and ends. Markets ring the institutions, and the methods are the hub, the tools every other ring is read with. Part 1 built this wheel one stage at a time, and five of its stages added a tool to the hub: optimization in Stage 2, strategy in Stage 4, equilibrium in Stage 5, measurement in Stage 7 and history in Stage 8. The plan below is the wheel cut open and laid flat. Each row is one ring.

Folded, the view steps back from the plan, and the wheel is built ring by ring beside the page. The agents come first, then the markets, through which goods, work and money start to circulate, then the institutions, and last the methods at the hub. It is the wheel the folded pages of Parts 1 and 2 lie on. Each cell holds what one course knows about one thing, and its name is a door to that course.

The economy is one wheel, and every figure in a textbook is drawn on part of it. The methods at the hub are not part of what is studied. They are how it is studied.

2What a model is

2A model moves some of the wheel, holds some still, and leaves the rest out.

No textbook figure draws the whole wheel. A model picks part of it and works out how that part moves. On the wheel, a model gives every cell one of three states.

  • Moving: what the model solves for. In Figure 1.1, the goods market, with its buyers, its sellers and the price.
  • Held still: what it takes as given, at a value. In Figure 1.1, income, held at 1, and with it the households and firms behind the two curves.
  • Left out: what it does not contain. Figure 1.1 has no banks, no government and no other countries.

Two more things complete a model: the method at the hub that moves its moving parts, and the assumptions it makes about the agents in them (section 4).

Part 1 named under every figure what the figure holds fixed, and folding the page raised that quantity as a third dimension. In twenty-two of its thirty figures the hidden axis is the value of a held cell: income in Figure 1.1, the interest rate in Figure 2.3, the price level in Figure 6.2. In four it is what the method climbs, read as contour lines: utility in Figures 2.1 and 5.1, profit in 4.1, the central bank’s loss in 6.4. Alfred Marshall described the method of holding in his Principles of Economics (1890): the causes that would disturb a question are set aside “in a pound called Cæteris Paribus”. On the wheel, that pound is the hatched cells.

The two models differ in what they move, and so in what they hold. IS–LM moves goods and money at once and holds the price level, which is the quantity Figure 1.1 solved for in its one market. Folded, the wheel beside the page takes the state of each model in turn. The routes through a held cell stop, with their parcels where they were, and the cells left out empty to their outline. Opening a held cell sets its routes moving again.

A model is a choice of what moves, what is held and what is left out. Most hidden axes in Part 1 are the value of what a model holds.

3How models are related

3Five relations join the models: open, add, relax, measure, return.

Part 1 wrote beside every figure what it builds on and where it returns, and Part 2 wrote what each section relaxes. Each of these is a change in the state of the wheel, and there are five kinds.

A course is a sequence of such changes. The classical sequence of Part 1 starts with one market moving and the rest held or left out. Each stage opens, adds, relaxes or measures one thing, until the whole wheel moves.

Stage 1 moves the goods market and holds the households and firms behind its curves. Stages 2 and 3 open them. Stage 4 changes no cell and relaxes an assumption instead. Stage 5 opens income, the last held cell, so that every market moves at once. Stages 6 and 8 add what was left out, and Stage 7 measures what the moving wheel produces. Two cells never move: networks and computation. Part 2 is where they are built. Folded, the wheel takes the state of each stage in turn, and the route of Part 1 is drawn through it figure by figure.

The core is the shortest sequence that sets the whole wheel moving. A field course goes one change beyond it.

4The assumptions

4Part 1 relaxes six of its eight assumptions itself. The other two are Part 2.

A model’s assumptions are claims about the agents in its moving cells: how many of them stand for their kind, who can reach whom, whether they can move the price, what they know. Eight run through Part 1. Figure 3.4 marks where each is made and where it is relaxed.

Six of the eight are relaxed inside the classical sequence. Price-taking gives way in Stage 4, full information in Figure 5.3, complete markets in Figure 5.2 and two field courses, consistent choice in Figure 8.9, equilibrium at once in Figure 6.3, and the claim that data show relationships in Stage 7. Two are never relaxed head-on. The representative agent lets one household or one firm stand for all of its kind. It is touched once, in Figure 8.10, where missing credit makes the distribution of wealth decide investment. The assumption of no connections lets anyone trade with anyone. These are the two that Part 1’s last section names, and their opposites are the title of Part 2: unlike and connected.

Folded, the assumptions show on the agents of the wheel. Drawn as a representative agent assumes, every firm is the same size. Relaxed, the firms are as unequal as Zipf’s law says, and households differ in income. Drawn without connections, any household buys from any firm through the market. Relaxed, each is tied to a few, and what moves travels along the ties.

Part 2 is not a separate economics. It relaxes the two assumptions the classical sequence keeps to the end.

5A unit of the textbook

5A unit is one model, written in nine fields.

If what a textbook teaches is models, a unit of the textbook is one model, and it can be written down in the same nine fields every time. Part 1 already writes most of them beside each figure.

Five rules arrange the units into a textbook.

  1. A unit makes one change: it opens one cell, adds one, or relaxes one assumption.
  2. A unit comes after the units it builds on. A cell must be built before it can be opened, and moving before it can be relaxed.
  3. The core is the shortest sequence that sets the whole wheel moving under the classical assumptions. In Part 1 it is Stages 1 to 7.
  4. A field course is one step beyond the core: it adds one thing or relaxes one assumption, as each course of Stage 8 does.
  5. A modern field is a step along an assumption, and can leave the core at any stage. Part 2 is made of such steps.
All thirty-seven units of Parts 1 and 2, in four of the nine fields

Folded, the wheel shows the card one field at a time: the cell the unit moves, the cells it holds at their values, the cell it opens, the agents as it assumes them, and the tool at the hub.

Written in the same nine fields, units can be put in a different order without losing what each one assumes.

6Five ways through

6Paths, not only courses.

Each path below enters the wheel somewhere else and visits the cells it needs in its own order. A question, an agent or a market enters at a cell. An assumption follows a property of the agents from cell to cell, and a method starts at the hub.

The paths by question, agent and market follow cells. The path by an assumption follows what Part 2 relaxes: it starts where Figure 1.1 makes the assumption of no connections and ends where a loss travels between banks. The path by a method stays at one tool of the hub while it moves around the rim, from the household to the firm, the central bank and the investor.

A course is one path among several. The archive keeps the classical sequence as one of them, without making it the only way in.

7The textbooks

7Every door, and what to read next.

The archive is a proposal, not a finished machine. Its cells are the courses that already exist, read through the models they teach. What it adds is the wheel that shows what each model moves, holds and leaves out, and how the models connect.
